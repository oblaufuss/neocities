TMBG on Gender

What does They Might Be Giants believe about gender?

We could ask the band directly, of course. In interviews, Johns Flansburgh and Linnell have always expressed disinterest in the machismo and sexism that runs through a lot of rock culture. (This may be why They've attracted a pretty gender-diverse following over the years; though the way They/Them pronouns feature prominently in the band's branding can't have hurt, either.) Or we could look through the Gender Issues theme on TMBW and draw some conclusions there.

But I believe that even more interesting than an artist's intentional statements on social issues are the work's unintentional ones: what seems so obvious that it surely doesn't need explanation? This page is a place to for musing on the patterns between songs, considering how they might fit together and reveal underlying philosophies.

Women & Men: Gender as Linguistic Category

Songs written by John Linnell are usually quite gender-neutral. Gender is, outside of the rare private romantic context, mostly only useful to note when a new set of pronouns introduces a new character. Female and male characters are balanced in number and importance. (In gender theory terms, this is something like the philosophy of equality feminism.)

Most flaws, goals, and anxieties are universal. In these songs we're often introduced to two or more characters over the course of several verses, each one with different pronouns but all representing instances of the same experience or idea:

  • As in Dog, "He's/She's the kind of dog..."
  • As in No Answer, "Though she may wonder; He'll always wonder";
  • As in Whistling In The Dark, "A woman/man came up to me and said..."
  • As in Certain People I Could Name, "Is he/she/it reminding you of anyone we know?"
  • As in Tractor, "Try though she may she'll never peel away your soul; He's met his match in your uncompromising ways";
  • As in Withered Hope, where "she" (Withered Hope), "he" (Sad Sack) and even "it" (the motorbike) form a loop of unrequited love.

Gender differentiates between unnamed figures as they interact. These male-female pairs are usually assumed to be in some (troubled) romantic dynamic, though the trouble between them rarely seems to hang on any difference in gendered roles:

  • As in Unrelated Thing's implied couple, "Said the woman to the man";
  • As in They'll Need A Crane, "Lad's gal is all he has, gal's gladness hangs upon the love of lad";
  • As in This Is Only Going To Go One Way's mutually murderous couple;
  • As in New Hampshire, which plays with this a bit by losing track of the number of speakers—"Woman wonders who's your itchy friend, woman says I though he was with you, woman says I thought he was with you".

For example...

The song Women and Men is, on its surface, probably the most immediately identifiable song that John Linnell has ever written "about gender". Examine it closer, though, and within the boundaries of this song gender practically doesn't exist.

The singer's gender or sex is irrelevant. The narration is detached, impartial. The world is linguistically divided in two, but these categories are only ever mentioned together, as halves of a whole. No statement about love or human behavior is exclusive to one or the other. If there are social or even biological difference in roles in reproduction or child-rearing, it's none of our business; that happens off in the jungle. If there's a difference in roles in sailing a ship, the narrator hasn't noticed either. It's most accurate to say that "woman" and "man" refer to sex here: the only remotely "gendered" assumption is that romantic pairings lead to sexual reproduction.

Ultimately, this song is really an observation on population growth and migration. Both sexes are, objectively, equally necessary for this in a structural way.

How Can I Sing Like A Girl?: Gender as Social Action

In songs written by John Flansburgh, gender tends to be much more character-defining. Someone's gender is described in terms of their behavior, obligations, or plans, particularly when relating to the opposite gender. (In actual gender theory terms, this is something like the philosophy of gender performativity.)

To be a "man" is to take on a job title, particularly when it comes to romance. Gender is a role that might have to be learned, a social expectation that requires work, an obligation that can be failed:

  • As in Narrow Your Eyes, "I don't want to shake your father's hand and act like a man";
  • As in Authenticity Trip, "Now I'm in these classes just to be your man";
  • As in You'll Miss Me, "It must be raining 'cause a man ain't supposed to cry";
  • As in Half A Boy, "Half a boy and nothing else; I wanna make promises I can't keep";
  • As in Too Tall Girl, "There's no kind of guidebook or catalog; to get to her a boy's got to calculate";
  • As in Extra Savoir-Faire (a French term which literally translates to know-how), "I know just what to do when the ladies come 'round; what is left for me to prove?"

But if gender is earned by plans of action, then it can be (at least temporarily) mutable. It's usually female characters in these songs trying on male roles as a form of fun:

  • As in Out Of Jail, "I could say she's to blame, say she's the man of this cautionary tale";
  • As in S-E-X-X-Y, "She wants to be your man, she's got another plan";
  • As in Damn Good Times, "She acts like David Lee Roth when he turned 21".

And characters in these songs, particularly the women, may enlist help from the cameraderie of same-gender relationships when planning their course of action towards the opposite gender:

  • As in It's Fun To Steal, "In the ladies' room you're all they're talking about";
  • As in Operators Are Standing By, "Making jokes about their old boyfriends (That's enough talking, ladies)";
  • As in Take Out The Trash, "Girl, why not take out the trash? I'm not saying all the boys are the same, but some boys are the same";
  • As in the advice in Too Tall Girl being delivered to the "boy" by the chorus of "men".

For example...

The song How Can I Sing Like A Girl is probably the most immediately identifiable song "about gender" that John Flansburgh has written, and it's quite literally about a performance.

The song hangs on the idea that even outside the world of sex and romance, gender expectations mean that an action can be done "like a girl" regardless of who is doing it. This is not a symmetrical equation. Being seen as girl-like (or simply as a girl) comes with social consequences, ridicule, objectification. The song's meaning would shift significantly if we didn't see the singer as a man.

The narrator approaches this social problem looking for a social solution: seeking advice from the listener. The "how?" in the title isn't asking if it's possible to act against type, but instead anxiously wondering if there's a way to get away with it. The potential solution presented is to find cameraderie with those who have shared gender-goals.

Lists of Pairs: Even Numbers / Mr. Hughes Says

While these songs might sound like an odd pair for comparison—one off a 2000s kids' educational album, the other off a 90s experimental solo album—both are structured around listing pairs of things, and the pairs chosen provide a particularly clear illustration of the "neutral category"/"meaningful action" approaches to gender.

Leave out the odd; you're left with the even
Skip all the odd ones

Skip the first number and the second one's even
Skip the third and the next one's even as well

Take, skip, take

Girl, boy, girl

Yes, no, yes, no, yes, no, yes, no, red, green

She loves me, she loves me not, she loves me
She loves me not, she

from Mr. Hughes Says

Tell your sister and your brother
Tell the alien and the chimp
Tell the corpse and tell the killer
Tell the pilot and the blimp

Tell the wind and tell the phone
Tell the birds and tell the fly
Tell the streetlights and the car
Tell the stars and tell the sky

Mr. Hughes says, "Live and learn.
Dig and be dug in return."

Tell the boxer and the fist
Tell the logo and the lid
Tell the ladies and the lords
Tell the goats and tell the kid

Tell the stylus and the disc
Tell the dust and tell the breeze
Tell the needles and the wool
Tell the locks and tell the keys

Even Numbers includes "girl/boy" as one of its examples of equal opposites: even/odd, red/green, yes/no. Like when pronouns are used as dialogue tags to keep track of a conversation, the subjects' genders alternate back and forth with mathematical precision. ("You're a girl but you're a boy" expects this equal-oppositeness to be so obvious that the listener understands it's addressing two different people, but that wouldn't be a given from a mutable-gender perspective!)

Gender isn't completely irrelevant to this song about basic math; the "she" in "she loves me not" is presumably picked because we know that the singer is a "he". But it's still only the symmetry of the pair that matters, not which half does the action. It's easy to imagine that if sung by a "she", the lyric could be swapped to "he loves me not" without any sense of subversion or shift in meaning.

Mr. Hughes Says uses much more poetic pairings. Most of these pairs not only act upon each other, but actually require one another to be able to act: pilot/blimp, corpse/killer, boxer/fist, stylus/disc, needles/wool, locks/keys. It would be bizarre to argue that most of these are either "equal" or "opposite" (in fact, goat/kid describes two stages of the same exact thing) and so it follows that the gender pairs of "brother/sister" and "lady/lord" don't actually have to be seen as opposing categories, either.

The chorus of "Dig and be dug in return" emphasizes the active, reciprocal relationships between these pairs. And while the song has been clearly stated to be about love, just because a pair's functional roles fit together doesn't mean their love should automatically be understood in the romantic sense: goat/kid and sister/brother, for example.
